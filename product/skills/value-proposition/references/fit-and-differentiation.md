# Fit Check and Differentiation

Read this when scoring the fit between a draft value proposition and its segment, or when proving that it is different from the real alternatives.

## Ranking jobs, pains and gains

- **Pains** by severity: frequency × intensity × cost (time, money, risk, reputation). A daily mild annoyance can outrank a rare disaster, or the reverse; write down which.
- **Gains** by type (Osterwalder's categories): *required* (no value without it), *expected* (assumed), *desired* (asked for), *unexpected* (beyond expectations). Required and expected gains are parity; desired and unexpected gains are where difference lives.
- **Jobs**: functional jobs explain what they do; emotional and social jobs often explain why they choose. A value proposition that ignores them loses to a functionally worse product that makes the buyer look good.

## Scoring rubric

| Score | Meaning |
|-------|---------|
| 0 | Not addressed |
| 1 | Partly reduced or partly delivered, or fully addressed but with no evidence yet |
| 2 | Removed or fully delivered, with evidence (pilot, usage data, customer quotes after use) |

### Bands (on-paper fit)

| Fit | Reading |
|-----|---------|
| ≥ 75% | Strong problem-solution fit on paper; test whether behaviour confirms it |
| 50-75% | Partial; name the gaps and decide whether to close them or narrow the segment |
| < 50% | Wrong segment, or the solution is not ready for this segment |

On-paper fit is not product-market fit. Product-market fit is shown by behaviour: customers choose, pay, use and stay.

### Flags to report

- **Orphan features:** product elements with no line to a ranked pain or gain. They cost money and dilute messaging; leave them out of this value proposition.
- **Unrelieved top pains:** a top-3 pain with no reliever is a gap, or a sign that this is the wrong segment.
- **Wrong-problem focus:** most lines go to pains or gains ranked 4 or lower.
- **Unevidenced relief:** scores of 1 because evidence is missing become value hypotheses.

## Differentiation

### Alternatives table

| Alternative | Type | What it does well (honest) | Where it fails this segment | Switching cost from it | Our edge (evidence) |
|-------------|------|----------------------------|-----------------------------|------------------------|---------------------|
| | Status quo / DIY / hire someone / adjacent tool / direct competitor | | | | |

Rules:
- Always include the status quo (doing nothing, the spreadsheet, the manual process). For many segments it is the main competitor.
- Be honest about what each alternative does well; customers already know, and a value proposition that pretends otherwise loses credibility in the first sales call.
- Group similar alternatives ("general-purpose spreadsheets"), as product-positioning does.

### Switching costs

| Type | Examples | How the offer can lower it |
|------|----------|----------------------------|
| Procedural | Learning, setup, migration, changing routines | Import, templates, done-for-you setup, gradual rollout |
| Financial | Contract exit fees, sunk licences, new spend | Pricing aligned to renewal dates, credits, free tier |
| Relational / emotional | Loyalty to a vendor or person, fear of blame if it fails | References from peers, guarantees, pilot before commitment |

### Parity vs difference

- **Points of parity** are what the segment expects from anything in the category. Missing one gets you excluded; exceeding one rarely wins. Check parity items with Kano must-be results if available.
- **Points of difference** are attributes where you are better or unique **and** the segment ranks that dimension in its top three. Being unique on something nobody ranks highly is not differentiation.
- To find candidates, list unique attributes and run each through the so-what chain (attribute → what it enables → why the customer cares); product-positioning covers this in depth.

## The swap test

Put the main alternative's name in place of yours in the value statement. If the sentence is still true, it is not differentiated.

- Weak: "[Product] helps small agencies manage invoices easily." Every invoicing tool passes the swap, so the statement says nothing.
- Strong: "[Product] chases overdue invoices for small agencies in their own voice and stops the moment a client pays, so owners stop losing Friday afternoons to payment reminders." The swap fails for a generic invoicing tool, so the statement carries a real difference.

## Value curve and ERRC grid

A value curve (from Kim and Mauborgne's strategy canvas) compares offerings across the factors the segment uses to choose.

1. List 6-10 factors from customer evidence, including price and switching effort.
2. Score each offering 1-5 on each factor, from the customer's point of view.
3. Draw or tabulate the curves. Curves that run parallel to the alternatives mean no differentiation.
4. Apply ERRC: which factors to **eliminate**, **reduce**, **raise** and **create** so the curve diverges where the segment cares most.

| Factor | Spreadsheet + email | Generic invoicing tool | Our product |
|--------|---------------------|------------------------|-------------|
| Price | 5 | 3 | 3 |
| Setup effort (higher = easier) | 4 | 3 | 4 |
| Time saved on chasing | 1 | 3 | 5 |
| Automated reminders in the owner's own voice | 1 | 1 | 4 |
| Visibility of who owes what | 2 | 3 | 5 |
| Invoice design options | 2 | 5 | 2 |

ERRC read-out: **reduce** invoice design (parity is enough), **raise** time saved on chasing and visibility, **create** automated reminders that sound like the owner (no alternative offers both automation and the owner's voice), and **keep** setup effort close to the spreadsheet's. Nothing needs eliminating here; in crowded categories, eliminating a factor the segment does not value is often the cheapest way to diverge.
